Two Sides of One Coin: Sustainability and Sovereignty in the Age of AI

By Akhila Matathammal•November 10, 2026•9 min read

On 9 June 2026, Anthropic released two of the most capable AI models in the world, Fable 5 and Mythos 5. Three days later, both stopped working for every customer, in every country. A US government export-control directive, citing national security, required the company to suspend access for foreign nationals, and the company switched both models off for everyone while it complied (Anthropic). The models were simply no longer available, because of a decision taken by another country’s government that none of their users had any part in.

On the other hand, consider what has happened in Visakhapatnam (India) this year. Google is building a $15 billion AI data centre hub there, and residents worried about scarce water and power have protested under the slogan “cannot drink data”. Near Mumbai (Thane, India), residents are fighting an Amazon data centre over the same fears. Here the question is not whether AI can be switched off, but whether a country can afford to keep it running: whose water, whose power, and who decides.

Now put the two stories side by side. In Visakhapatnam and Thane, the land, the water and the power are India’s. The companies are American, and so is the law they answer to. That is the same law under which, two AI models were switched off overnight for every customer in the world. Look, too, at where the maker of those models is planning its own infrastructure outside the US. In September 2026, Anthropic signed its first data centre lease in Australia, as the anchor tenant of a gigawatt-scale campus, and it is hiring deal-makers to secure data centre capacity across Europe. So any country that hosts AI infrastructure built by others, whether in Europe, in Australia or in India, spends its own water and power on AI whose availability to its own people is decided elsewhere. Who, then, is that sustainability cost being paid for?

That is the bridge between the two stories, and it is what this article is about. The first story is about sovereignty: control over your own decisions. The second is about sustainability, which software engineers describe as the capacity of a system to keep going across five dimensions, environmental, economic, social, individual and technical, none of which can be treated in isolation. Without sovereignty, a country can end up paying the sustainability cost of AI it does not control. Without sustainability, the AI it does control cannot be kept running.

Is this link new to the AI age? AI is the latest strategic resource a country depends on, but it is not the first. To see where AI is heading, we take India as our example. Few countries have lived through this tension between depending on others and controlling their own supply as often, or as visibly, as India has. Its record in food, medicines and energy shows what happens when a country takes these decisions into its own hands, and its position in chips shows what is still at stake in AI.

India has been here before

In the 1960s, India’s food came from American wheat under the US PL-480 programme. When drought struck in 1965, President Lyndon Johnson put that supply on a “short tether”, approving shipments only two months, then one month, at a time. India answered by growing its own. The Green Revolution more than doubled wheat output, from about 12 million tonnes in 1965 to over 26 million tonnes by 1972, fed a growing nation and ended the dependence. When intensive farming later drained Punjab’s groundwater, India corrected course on its own terms, with a 2009 law estimated to save 276 million units of electricity and about Rs 122 crore a year, along with the water.

In medicines, India chose to write its own patent rules. Today it supplies nearly 20% of the world’s generic medicines, and HIV treatment fell from over $10,000 to about $150 per patient per year. The US still keeps India on its intellectual-property watch list, yet India holds its ground, because the world, the US included, now relies on the industry those rules built.

In energy, India imports about 88% of its crude oil, a bill of $90.6 billion in just six months of 2026. So it turned to its own sunlight, and solar capacity grew from 2.82 GW in 2014 to 162 GW by June 2026. India reached 50% non-fossil power capacity five years ahead of its Paris target, its emissions grew by just 0.7% in 2025, the slowest in two decades, and nobody can switch off the sun.

Three resources, one pattern: each time India took the decision into its own hands, the result was more sustainable, and the sustainable result made India harder to coerce.

AI runs on two resources where that pattern has not yet played out: electricity and chips.

“There is no AI without energy – specifically electricity.”

Fatih Birol, Executive Director, International Energy Agency, February 2025

India is working on the electricity. The chips are another matter. One American company, Nvidia, sells most of the world’s AI accelerators: IDC puts its share at about 81%. The most advanced chips can only be made with machines from a single Dutch company, ASML (SEMI), and who may buy those machines is decided by governments: under US pressure, the Netherlands has never allowed ASML to ship them to China (Taipei Times).

India has felt the same lever. In January 2025, the US AI Diffusion Rule placed India in its second tier, capping imports at about 50,000 advanced GPUs through 2027. The rule was withdrawn in May 2025, but it showed where the switch sits. Even the GPUs that India has pooled for its national AI compute mission, the backbone of its sovereign AI plans, are imported. Nvidia’s own chief executive has made the case for owning this layer:

“Every country needs to own the production of their own intelligence.”

Jensen Huang, CEO of Nvidia, at the World Governments Summit, Dubai, February 2024

Depending on others for chips also limits how sustainable India’s AI can be. Whoever designs and sells the hardware decides how much power it draws, what it costs and whether supply continues, and India has little room for those decisions to go wrong. The country generates nearly 20% of the world’s data but hosts only about 3% of its data centres, and its data centres, which used about 150 billion litres of water in 2024–25, may need nearly 358 billion litres a year by 2030. India has started to respond. IT minister Ashwini Vaishnaw said in 2025 that India would build its own GPU within three to four years, and in early 2026 he held talks with Nvidia on developing sovereign GPUs in India.

What this means in the AI era

Each story has a direct counterpart in AI today.

  • Food then, models now. In 1966, India’s grain arrived a month at a time, on someone else’s terms. In June 2026, the most capable AI models were switched off for three weeks, on someone else’s terms.
  • Medicines then, rules now. India made medicines affordable by writing its own patent rules. The rules of AI, on data, model licenses and where computing must sit, are being written now.
  • Energy then, data centers now. India’s own solar kept money at home and cut emissions. Today, India’s water and power are being committed to AI infrastructure that others build and govern.
  • Chips, then and now. In AI hardware, India stands where it stood with food in 1965.

Every time India took the decision into its own hands, the outcome was more sustainable, and harder for anyone to reverse. Every time it depended on others, it paid twice: once in control, and again in cost.

This is where the popular advice to build only on top falls short. Nandan Nilekani has told Indian start-ups that “Our goal should not be to build one more LLM”. Building on top is how AI reaches people. But building only on top means owning everything except the foundation, and in June 2026 every application built on two models lost its foundation in a day. The government’s own white paper is right to call for capability across the foundation-model stack.

Others are already acting on this. Europe is mobilising €200 billion for AI and has opened bids for seven AI gigafactories to cut its reliance on American and Chinese computing, alongside a proposed Chips Act 2.0. Australia ties data centre investment to the energy transition and sustainable water use. Ireland makes data centres report their emissions and renewable energy use. None of them treats control and sustainability as a trade-off.

The sovereignty-sustainability trilemma, the claim that no country can maximise sovereignty, minimise environmental impact and keep costs affordable at once, therefore measures the wrong thing. It counts the cost of pursuing sovereignty carelessly, not the cost of sovereignty itself. Its own authors come close to saying so:

“Sovereign AI without environmental accounting is incomplete sovereignty.”

Syed and colleagues, The Environmental Cost of Digital Sovereignty, 2026

A trade-off is a luxury for those who already own the stack. For everyone else, the real cost is not being sovereign and sustainable.

Conclusion

Sovereignty and sustainability are not at loggerheads. They are one loop. Sovereignty is the power to make your own decisions. Sustainability is the ability to keep those decisions going, across the environment, the economy, society, individuals and technology. Without the first, a country cannot choose the sustainable path. Without the second, it cannot hold on to the choices it made. India has run this loop with food, medicines and energy. AI is the next place to run it.

India is our example, but the conclusion holds for every country. For AI, it means sovereignty across the full stack, hardware included, built sustainably so that it lasts:

  1. Build your own AI hardware. GPUs, chip design and fabrication are the layer where most countries, India among them, are most exposed. Treat them as the Green Revolution of this era.
  2. Own the models and data that essential services run on, so that no switch-off abroad can stop a hospital, a bank or a school at home.
  3. Build frugally. Adapt existing models before training new ones, power data centers with clean energy and site them away from water-stressed regions, and share compute as public infrastructure, as India’s national AI mission does with its GPU pool.
  4. Until the stack is complete, depend on several partners, never on one.

A country that controls its AI can choose to make it sustainable, and AI that is sustainable is AI a country can keep controlling. That is the loop India has closed before, and the one it now needs to close for AI.
